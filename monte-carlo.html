Monte Carlo Mössbauer Ray-Tracer

A photon simulation for finding the best source, sample, and detector geometry in a miniaturized Mössbauer spectrometer — and for quantifying exactly what you give up when you can't have the ideal one.

Background

The geometry problem

Mössbauer spectroscopy is built on a narrow nuclear resonance — Fe-57 absorbs gamma rays at 14.4 keV with extraordinary specificity, making it one of the best tools for identifying iron-bearing minerals. On Earth, instruments can be large. The source, sample, and detector can be arranged at leisure.

On a Mars lander, that luxury disappears. Mass budgets are tight, and the instrument needs to work in backscatter — source and detector on the same side as the sample, aimed at the rock face rather than through it. The geometry gets compact, and compact geometry introduces a problem: photons don't all travel the same path. Some scatter. Some enter the detector at oblique angles. The velocity axis of the Doppler drive — what makes Mössbauer spectroscopy work — gets smeared.

You can collect more counts by opening up the geometry. But more counts at the cost of a distorted spectrum isn't always a good trade.

Method

How the simulator works

Step 01

Spawn photons

Photons are generated at the source with random directions sampled from the emission distribution. Each photon carries a velocity — the Doppler shift the detector will see — determined by its angle of incidence relative to the drive axis.

Step 02

Trace paths

Each photon is propagated through the geometry: source → sample → detector. Photons that miss the detector are discarded. Those that reach it are binned by their effective Doppler velocity.

Step 03

Build the smearing kernel

The distribution of detected velocities for a monochromatic input is the smearing kernel — a fingerprint of how much the geometry distorts the spectrum. A perfect geometry gives a delta function. Real geometries spread it out.

Step 04

Correct the data

The kernel can be convolved into a model spectrum to simulate what a real detector would see — or used to deconvolve an experimental spectrum back toward the true signal. This lets you fit data from non-ideal geometries without ignoring the distortion.

Application

Why this matters for miniaturization

The goal of the broader Mössbauer miniaturization project is a spectrometer small enough to fly on next-generation Mars landers and lunar surface missions without dominating the mass and power budget. At those scales, the ideal transmission geometry is simply not available — the detector has to sit beside the source, and the sample geometry is whatever the planet hands you.

The simulator makes it possible to explore the design space before anything is built: compare geometries, quantify the count-rate versus distortion tradeoff for each, and generate the correction kernel that turns the chosen geometry's raw data into something you can actually fit. You don't have to pick between sensitivity and interpretability — you just have to know what you're working with.
