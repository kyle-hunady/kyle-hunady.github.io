The entropy of high-entropy alloys

I measured the heat capacity of eight alloys down to 2 K to find out where their entropy comes from and how aluminum changes it.

High-entropy alloys get their name from the many ways different elements can share a crystal lattice. Does that mixing explain how stable the alloys are? I measured the entropy from heat capacity and used diffraction and microscopy to estimate how much came from mixing. In these alloys, atomic vibrations accounted for most of the entropy, and adding aluminum lowered the total.

I studied three alloy families based on CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeMnNi and NbTa0.5TiZr, with and without added aluminum. I published the study with Ihab Elmasri and Brent Fultz in Acta Materialia (2026).

IEntropy and stability

What is entropy?

There are many microscopic ways to arrange a material while keeping its composition and other bulk properties the same. In Boltzmann’s formula, S = kB ln W, W is the number of accessible microscopic states and kB is Boltzmann’s constant. Here I report entropy in kB per atom, equivalent to about 8.3 J/(mol·K) for each kB per atom. For a perfect crystal of one element, there’s only one way to fill the lattice sites, so its configurational entropy is zero. Adding another element gives the atoms many more ways to occupy those sites.

modelA 50/50 mix has the most possible arrangements. Each shuffle on the left places the same number of gold atoms on different sites. With 20 gold atoms and 40 sites, there are about 1.4 × 1011 possible arrangements. A mole of atoms has on the order of 1023 sites.

At a fixed temperature and pressure, the stable state has the lowest Gibbs free energy, G = H − TS. Stronger bonds can lower the enthalpy H. Higher entropy S lowers G through the −TS term, and this contribution grows with temperature. A state with higher enthalpy can therefore become stable when the temperature rises.

In each example, entropy helps determine which state is stable. A similar argument motivated the name high-entropy alloy. Mixing more elements gives atoms more ways to occupy the lattice, which can favor a disordered solid solution over an ordered compound.

Four contributions to entropy

The gold-and-blue model shows configurational entropy, which comes from how atoms share lattice sites. Three other contributions matter in metals. Atoms vibrate, electrons occupy different energy states, and magnetic moments fluctuate.

datamodelIn CoCrFeMnNi at 385 K, ideal mixing accounts for about a quarter of the total. Ideal mixing gives 1.61 kB per atom. Calorimetry gives 4.71 kB per atom for the other three contributions combined. Density functional theory (DFT) puts the electronic contribution at 0.16 kB per atom.

Can mixing stabilize a solid solution?

Metals often form ordered compounds because some pairs of elements bond more strongly than others. In 2004, two groups showed that certain alloys with five or more principal elements could form simple solid solutions (Yeh 2004, Cantor 2004). Yeh proposed that configurational entropy could help explain this. For n elements mixed randomly in equal proportions, the contribution is kB ln n per atom. At sufficiently high temperatures, this entropy can outweigh the enthalpy advantage of an ordered compound.

modelThe solid solution is favored where its free energy (green) is below that of the ordered compound (red). In this model, adding a sixth element moves the crossover temperature a little. A vibrational entropy difference of a few tenths of kB per atom, comparable to what I measured, can move it by hundreds of kelvin.

Alloys with the same ideal mixing entropy can form different phases

Otto and co-workers started with the single-phase Cantor alloy, CoCrFeMnNi, and replaced one element at a time. Each alloy kept the same number of elements in equal proportions, so they all had the same ideal configurational entropy (Otto 2013).

What B2 ordering looks like

The formula kB ln n assumes that the elements mix randomly. In AlCoCrFeNi, aluminum and nickel prefer one set of sites on the body-centered cubic (BCC) lattice. The cube corners and centers then have different compositions, giving the ordered B2 structure, the same pattern as CsCl. The site preferences reduce the number of possible arrangements.

modelIn the B2 model, Al and Ni (gold and blue) prefer corner sites, while Co, Cr and Fe favor the centers. Move the slider toward random mixing to see configurational entropy rise. Al and Ni make up only 40% of the atoms, so Co occupies some corner sites even at full order. The model gives 1.05 kB per atom at full order and 1.61 for random mixing. Ordering also changes how the sites scatter X-rays, producing an additional diffraction peak. From its intensity, I estimated 1.31 kB per atom for the measured alloy.

What formed in the samples

I checked every alloy for the B2 diffraction peak. I also polished and imaged the samples to look for regions with different compositions. Those regions can form as an alloy solidifies and reduce its configurational entropy.

dataTwo aluminum-rich alloys show B2 order, and microscopy reveals regions of different composition. The B2 {100} reflection (dashed line) appears in AlCoCrFeNi and Al1.25CoCrFeMnNi. The refractory alloys retain a disordered BCC structure but also show Nb–Ta-rich dendrites, which form as the melt freezes. The regions between them are rich in Ti and Zr or in Al and Zr. The two alloys with B2 order also show chemical segregation. Both ordering and segregation lower configurational entropy.

IIIHeat capacity measurements

Heat capacity down to 2 K

Heat capacity is the energy it takes to raise a sample’s temperature by a given amount. Integrating heat capacity divided by temperature from absolute zero gives the combined vibrational, electronic and magnetic entropy in these alloys. I measured heat capacity with a Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS), taking three measurements at each temperature from 2 to 400 K for all eight alloys.

dataThe measured entropy drops when aluminum is added in all three families. The right plot shows the integral of Cp/T from the left plot. At 385 K, the measured entropy is roughly three times kB ln 5, and the alloy with the most aluminum has the lowest measured entropy in each family.

How aluminum changes the entropy

Aluminum is light and forms relatively stiff, partly covalent bonds with the transition metals. Lower masses and stiffer bonds both raise vibrational frequencies, which lowers vibrational entropy. In each family, the loss is larger than the gain in ideal mixing entropy from adding aluminum.

dataAdding aluminum lowers total entropy in all three alloy families. The top plot compares measured and configurational entropy at 385 K. For the highest aluminum content in each family, ideal mixing adds 0.16–0.22 kB per atom and measured entropy falls by 0.34–0.44 kB per atom. B2 order further reduces configurational entropy in the two Cantor-type alloys. The net changes are −0.46, −0.62 and −0.17 kB per atom.

The 0.62 kB per atom lost in Al1.25CoCrFeMnNi is close to the change in ideal mixing entropy from going from six equally abundant elements to three (kB ln 2 ≈ 0.69 kB).

IVSimulating atomic vibrations

Because vibrations contribute most of the entropy, I wanted to see how much of the measured curve they could explain. Both simulation methods need to compute forces on atoms as their positions change. In these calculations, DFT takes minutes for each arrangement. I used MACE, a machine-learned model trained on DFT data, to compute forces for 160 atoms in a fraction of a second. I ran molecular dynamics and phonon calculations on the same cells.

Molecular dynamics

I started with 160 atoms of Al0.3NbTa0.5TiZr randomly distributed on a BCC lattice in a periodic cell. I gave each atom an initial velocity and computed the forces and motion in 2-femtosecond steps. Twenty thousand steps covered 40 picoseconds.

In classical molecular dynamics, the average kinetic energy per atom is (3/2) kBT. A thermostat controls the velocities to keep the system near the target temperature. The figure shows part of the simulation at three temperatures.

Its typical displacement increases from 0.07 Å at 50 K to 0.16 Å at 300 K, compared with 2.9 Å between neighboring atoms. The larger displacements are consistent with higher vibrational entropy.

Phonon calculations with phonopy

Phonopy derives vibrational frequencies from the forces caused by small atomic displacements (Togo 2015). I displaced each atom by 0.01 Å and used MACE to compute the forces on the other atoms. Those forces act like springs near equilibrium. Phonopy uses them to find the crystal’s vibration patterns and their frequencies. Softer bonds and heavier atoms lower the frequencies, which raises vibrational entropy.

The fitted vibrational frequencies changed little with temperature. I fitted force constants to the forces from molecular dynamics and compared them with phonopy. I used five cells in total for NbTa0.5TiZr with 0, 0.2 and 0.3 Al, including three random cells for the alloy without aluminum. At 300 K the average frequency differed by −0.4% to +0.9%. The frequencies would have to rise about 8% to match the measured heat capacity.

Comparing simulations with calorimetry

Magnetic and electronic contributions are absent from the calculation and account for much of the difference in the magnetic alloys. The force constants vary little with temperature in molecular dynamics, so the discrepancy likely comes from MACE underestimating bond stiffness.

What I found

Atomic vibrations contributed most of the entropy. Adding aluminum increased ideal mixing entropy, but it reduced vibrational entropy by a larger amount. For these alloys, predicting the stability of a disordered phase requires accounting for both contributions.

What I did

  • Measured heat capacity from 2 to 400 K for CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeMnNi and NbTa₀.₅TiZr alloys with and without added aluminum.
  • Characterized phases and chemical order with X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy and ⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy.
  • Used spin-polarized DFT to estimate electronic and magnetic contributions.
  • Ran phonon calculations and molecular dynamics with a MACE force field to compare with the heat capacity measurements.
  • Wrote code to calculate entropy and its uncertainty from raw calorimetry data.
