The entropy of high-entropy alloys

What the name assumes, why it might be wrong, and what calorimetry down to 2 K says about three families of high-entropy alloys.

All projects

Mix five metals in equal parts and you get a "high-entropy alloy." The name makes a claim: these alloys hold together because their atoms are so thoroughly mixed. I tested that claim by measuring the total entropy of eight of these alloys. Mixing turned out to be the smaller share, and adding aluminum as one more element lowered the total.

IThe idea

Four ways to be disordered

Entropy counts the ways a material can be arranged without anyone noticing a difference. A metal has four separate ways. Atoms can trade places on the lattice. They vibrate about their sites. Electrons near the top of the filled levels spread into empty ones, and magnetic moments wander. Each one adds its own entropy, and they add up.

dataThe term the alloys are named after is about a quarter of the total. For CoCrFeMnNi at 385 K, ideal mixing gives 1.61 kB per atom. Calorimetry gives 4.71 for the other three together, and DFT puts 0.16 of that in the electrons.

The bet behind the name

Mixtures of metals often form ordered compounds, because unlike atoms bond more strongly than like ones. In 2004 two groups melted five or more metals together in equal amounts and many of them came out as one simple crystal anyway (Yeh 2004; Cantor 2004). Yeh's explanation was configurational entropy. With n elements on random sites, each atom carries kB ln n. That term enters the free energy as −TS, so at high enough temperature it can outweigh the enthalpy the compound gains from bonding.

modelThe random alloy (green) wins wherever its line falls below the compound (red). Adding a sixth element moves the crossover a little. A vibrational difference of a few tenths of kB, the size I measured, moves it by hundreds of kelvin. So the bet depends on mixing being the only entropy difference between the two.

IIThe doubt

Same mixing entropy, different fates

If mixing entropy decided everything, any five-element alloy should behave like any other. Otto and co-workers checked this directly. They took the single-phase Cantor alloy and swapped out one element at a time, which keeps the element count, and so the mixing entropy, fixed (Otto 2013).

Ordering, drawn as a crystal structure

The formula kB ln n assumes every site is a free choice. Real atoms have preferences. Aluminum and nickel bond strongly, so in AlCoCrFeNi they collect on one set of sites. The crystal is still body-centered cubic, but the cube corners and the cube centers now hold different atoms. That is the B2 structure, the same pattern as CsCl. Each sublattice now has fewer kinds of atom to choose from, so there are fewer arrangements.

modelIn ordered B2, Al and Ni (gold, blue) hold the cube corners and Co, Cr and Fe the centers. Slide toward random to give the entropy back. Fully ordered, AlCoCrFeNi keeps 0.90 kB of its 1.61. Ordering also makes the corners and centers scatter X-rays differently, which creates a new diffraction peak. From the size of that peak I estimate 1.31 for the real alloy.

What actually formed

I looked for that peak in every alloy. I also polished and imaged each one, because the elements can separate into regions of different composition as the melt freezes, and that lowers the mixing entropy too.

dataWith enough aluminum the Cantor-type alloys order into B2, and the refractory alloys separate into regions. The B2 {100} peak (dashed line) appears only in AlCoCrFeNi and Al1.25CoCrFeMnNi. The refractory alloys stay plain BCC, but under the microscope they split into Nb–Ta-rich and Ti–Zr- or Al–Zr-rich regions. The aluminum-rich Cantor-type alloys separate too. Both effects pull the mixing entropy below kB ln n.

IIIThe measurement

Heat capacity down to 2 K

Configurational entropy is hard to measure, but the other three terms are not. Heat a sample a fraction of a kelvin and record how much energy it takes. That is the heat capacity. Divide it by temperature and add up the area from absolute zero, and you have the vibrational, electronic and magnetic entropy together. I did this for eight alloys in a PPMS calorimeter, three times at each temperature from 2 to 400 K.

dataAluminum pushes the entropy down in every family. Heat capacity (left) climbs toward 3 kB per atom. The area under Cp/T (middle) is the entropy (right). By 385 K it is roughly three times kB ln 5, and the aluminum-rich alloy is lowest each time.

The entropy budget

Aluminum is light, and its bonds to the transition metals are partly covalent. Both make the lattice stiffer, so the atoms vibrate faster and carry less entropy. In every family that loss is bigger than the mixing entropy aluminum brings.

dataAdding aluminum lowers the total entropy in all three families. Top: the measured terms are about three times the configurational one. Bottom: aluminum adds 0.16–0.22 kB of ideal mixing, but the measured entropy drops 0.34–0.44, and B2 order takes more in the Cantor-type alloys. Net: −0.46, −0.62 and −0.17 kB per atom.

The −0.62 kB in Al1.25CoCrFeMnNi is about the mixing entropy you would lose by cutting a six-element alloy down to three elements (kB ln 2 = 0.69).

Simulating the heat capacity

If vibrations carry most of the entropy, a phonon calculation should get most of the curve. I computed phonons with MACE, a machine-learned force field, on supercells with the alloy's elements placed at random. For the refractory alloy I also ran molecular dynamics at 50, 150 and 300 K to see whether the vibrations change with temperature.

measuredMACE phonons

simulationPhonons alone come within 0.25 kB of the refractory alloys and miss CoCrFeNi and CoCrFeMnNi by about half a kB. The force field has no magnetism and no electrons, and those two terms make up most of the gap in the magnetic alloys. At 50 K it runs 9–18 % high for the refractory alloys, so its phonons are a little too soft. The molecular dynamics shifted the vibrational frequencies by under 0.4 % at 300 K, so the error is in the force field, not in anharmonicity.

What I did

  • Measured heat capacity from 2 to 400 K on aluminum-added CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeMnNi and NbTa₀.₅TiZr.
  • Checked phases and chemical order with X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy and ⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy.
  • Ran spin-polarized DFT to separate the magnetic and electronic contributions.
  • Computed phonons and ran molecular dynamics with a MACE machine-learned force field to simulate the heat capacity.
  • Wrote the processing code that turns raw calorimeter files into entropy with uncertainty.
