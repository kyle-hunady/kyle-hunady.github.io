The entropy of high-entropy alloys

I measured the heat capacity of eight alloys down to 2 K to find out where their entropy comes from and how aluminum changes it.

Eight alloys in three families, based on CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeMnNi and NbTa0.5TiZr, each with and without added aluminum. I measured heat capacity from 2 to 400 K and used X-ray diffraction and electron microscopy to look for chemical order. Published with Ihab Elmasri and Brent Fultz in Acta Materialia (2026). The simulations in part IV came later and are not in the paper.
Most of the entropy in these alloys comes from atoms vibrating. At 385 K, vibrations, electrons and magnetism together give CoCrFeMnNi 4.71 kB per atom, about three times its ideal mixing entropy of 1.61. Aluminum raises the vibrational frequencies. In each family the alloy with the most aluminum lost 0.34–0.44 kB per atom of measured entropy, more than the 0.16–0.22 that aluminum adds through mixing, so the total fell in all three families.

IFour kinds of entropy

Counting arrangements

Entropy counts the microscopic arrangements a material can take while looking the same from outside. Boltzmann wrote it as S = kB ln W, where W is the number of arrangements and kB is Boltzmann’s constant. I give entropy in kB per atom; 1 kB per atom is 8.3 J/(mol·K).

modelA 50/50 mix has the most arrangements. Each shuffle on the left is one of the ways to put 20 gold atoms on 40 sites; there are about 1.4 × 1011 of them. Per site, the entropy approaches kB ln 2 (right).

Entropy picks the winner when it’s hot

At fixed temperature and pressure, the stable state has the lowest Gibbs free energy, G = H − TS. Strong bonds lower the enthalpy H. Entropy lowers G through the −TS term, which grows with temperature, so a state with more entropy can win once it is hot enough even if it costs energy.

Ice melts0 °CMelting costs about 6 kJ per mole. Liquid water has about 22 J/(mol·K) more entropy than ice, enough to favor the liquid above 273 K.
Iron loses its magnetism770 °CAligned magnetic moments have lower energy. Above the Curie temperature, the disordered moments win.
CuZn disordersAbout 460 °CBelow it, Cu and Zn sit on separate sites in the ordered B2 structure. Above it, they share both sets. Two of my aluminum-rich alloys have the same kind of order.

In each case the high-temperature state has more entropy. High-entropy alloys were named for the same argument.

Four kinds in a metal

The gold-and-blue model counts configurational entropy. A metal has three more kinds, and calorimetry measures those three together.

datamodelIn CoCrFeMnNi at 385 K, ideal mixing is about a quarter of the total. Five elements mixed at random give kB ln 5 = 1.61 kB per atom. Calorimetry gives 4.71 for the other three kinds together, of which density functional theory (DFT) puts 0.16 in the electrons.

IIMixing and chemical order

The high-entropy alloy idea

Metals often form ordered compounds because some pairs of elements bond more strongly than others. In 2004 two groups made alloys of five or more elements in near-equal amounts that formed simple solid solutions instead (Yeh 2004, Cantor 2004). Yeh’s explanation was mixing entropy. n elements mixed at random give kB ln n per atom, which at a high enough temperature can outweigh the compound’s better bonding.

modelVibrations can shift the balance more than an extra element. The solid solution (green) is stable where its free energy is below the ordered compound’s (red). With the starting settings, a sixth element lowers the crossover from 577 K to 518 K. Giving the solid solution 0.4 kB per atom less vibrational entropy than the compound, about the size of the drops I measured with aluminum, raises it to 768 K.

Same mixing entropy, different phases

Otto and co-workers started from the single-phase Cantor alloy, CoCrFeMnNi, and swapped out one element at a time. Every alloy kept five elements in equal amounts, so all six had the same ideal mixing entropy (Otto 2013).

B2 order: aluminum and nickel pick sides

The formula kB ln n assumes random mixing. In AlCoCrFeNi, aluminum and nickel prefer the corners of the body-centered cubic (BCC) cell, so corners and centers end up with different compositions. That is the B2 structure, the same pattern as CsCl, and it allows fewer arrangements.

modelB2 order can cut the mixing entropy of AlCoCrFeNi from 1.61 to 1.05 kB per atom. Ordering moves Al and Ni (gold and blue) onto the corners. They are only 40% of the atoms, so cobalt fills the remaining corner sites. The measured B2 diffraction peak puts the real alloy at 1.31 kB per atom or less.

What formed in the samples

I looked for the B2 diffraction peak in every alloy, then polished and imaged them to find regions of different composition that form as the melt freezes. Both lower the mixing entropy.

dataThe two aluminum-rich Cantor-type alloys order as B2. The refractory alloys stay unordered BCC but split into two regions as they freeze. The B2 {100} peak (dashed, left) appears only in AlCoCrFeNi and Al1.25CoCrFeMnNi. The segregation in the refractory alloys lowers the mixing entropy by only 0.03–0.05 kB per atom; B2 order lowers it by 0.30–0.36.

IIIMeasured entropy

Heat capacity from 2 to 400 K

Heat capacity Cp is the energy it takes to warm a sample by one degree. The area under Cp/T from absolute zero up to a temperature T is the entropy at T: here the vibrational, electronic and magnetic kinds together. I measured all eight alloys in a Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS) from 2 to 400 K, three times at each temperature.

Without aluminumMost aluminumEntropy lost, up to 385 K

dataAluminum delays the rise in heat capacity, and the shaded area is the entropy it removes. The aluminum-rich alloy reaches a given Cp/T at a higher temperature, a sign of higher vibrational frequencies. Up to 385 K, the highest temperature measured for all eight alloys, the shaded areas are 0.38, 0.44 and 0.34 kB per atom.

Where the entropy goes with aluminum

Aluminum is light and forms stiff, partly covalent bonds with the transition metals. Both raise the vibrational frequencies, and higher frequencies mean less vibrational entropy. In the paper, the low mass of aluminum accounts for about half of the drop.

Gain from ideal mixingMeasured changeOrdering or segregationNet change

dataAdding aluminum lowered the total entropy in all three families. For the alloy with the most aluminum in each family, ideal mixing gains 0.16–0.22 kB per atom and the measured entropy falls by 0.34–0.44. B2 order takes away more in the two Cantor-type alloys. The net changes are −0.46, −0.62 and −0.17 kB per atom. The 0.62 lost by Al1.25CoCrFeMnNi is close to what going from six equal elements to three would cost in mixing entropy (kB ln 2 = 0.69).

The measured entropy is 2.4 to 4.2 times the ideal mixing entropy in every alloy. Adding manganese to CoCrFeNi raised the measured entropy by 0.10 kB per atom, so the drop is specific to aluminum.

IVSimulated vibrations

I simulated the vibrations to see how much of the measured entropy they explain. Both methods below need the force on every atom as the atoms move. DFT takes minutes per arrangement; MACE, a machine-learned force field trained on DFT results, takes a fraction of a second for 160 atoms.

Molecular dynamics: what temperature means

I placed 160 atoms of Al0.3NbTa0.5TiZr at random on a BCC lattice in a periodic cell, gave each atom a starting velocity, and stepped Newton’s equations forward 2 femtoseconds at a time, 20,000 steps or 40 picoseconds per run. In a simulation, temperature measures how fast the atoms move: the average kinetic energy per atom is (3/2) kBT. A thermostat adjusts the velocities to hold the target temperature.

AlNbTaTiZr

simulationHotter atoms swing farther but stay close to their sites. Top: the full cell from the MACE run. Bottom: the path of one niobium atom over 3 picoseconds, at true scale. Its typical displacement grows from 0.07 Å at 50 K to 0.16 Å at 300 K, against 2.9 Å between neighbors. Swings this small are why a spring model of the bonds works.

Phonopy: vibrations from springs

Phonopy is an open-source code that finds a crystal’s vibrations from the forces caused by small displacements (Togo 2015). I moved each atom by 0.01 Å and used MACE for the forces on the other atoms. Near equilibrium those forces act like springs, and phonopy turns the springs into the crystal’s vibration patterns (modes) and their frequencies. Softer springs and heavier atoms give lower frequencies, and lower frequencies give more entropy.

model + simulationAt room temperature every mode carries entropy; near absolute zero only the slowest ones do. The histogram counts the 477 vibration modes of the 160-atom cell by energy; brighter bars carry more entropy at the chosen temperature.

Molecular dynamics found that the springs barely change with temperature. In five cells (NbTa0.5TiZr with 0, 0.2 and 0.3 Al, three arrangements of the aluminum-free alloy), the MD motion at 300 K gave frequencies within −0.4% to +0.9% of phonopy’s. Matching the measured low-temperature heat capacity of NbTa0.5TiZr would take frequencies about 14% higher, so temperature effects explain less than a tenth of that gap.

Simulation against calorimetry

MeasuredMACE phonons

datasimulationAt 385 K the phonon calculation comes within 0.02 kB per atom of NbTa0.5TiZr and falls about 0.6 short for CoCrFeMnNi. Neither calculation includes electrons or magnetism. Adding the 0.17 electronic term from DFT would leave the refractory calculation about 0.15 too high, consistent with springs that are too soft: at 50 K the calculated heat capacity of the refractory alloys is 9–18% too high, partly from MACE and mostly from the DFT it was trained on. For CoCrFeMnNi, electrons (0.16) and magnetism (about 0.14) close about half the gap.

What I did

  • Characterized phases and chemical order with X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy and ⁵⁷Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy.
