For a 3-D printed rocket combustion-chamber liner, GRCop-42 or GRCop-84?

Abstract
A summary of 6 published papers, with 207 values plotted from them (34 from tables, 7 from the text, 166 from digitized figures, plus 28 points of a published fit). 4 relevant papers could not be obtained.
Methods, in order: 1. tensile strength against temperature (wrought curves, printed room-temperature tests) → 2. thermal conductivity against temperature (wrought curves, printed laser-flash data, a published fit) → 3. room-temperature yield and tensile strength for every printed and wrought condition
TL;DR

GRCop-42, which is where NASA moved for printed chambers: GRCop-84 is stronger below about 600 °C, the two are matched above it, and GRCop-42 conducts 5-20 % more heat and prints about 20 % faster [1]. GRCop-42 (Cu-4Cr-2Nb, at%) carries half the Cr2Nb strengthening precipitate of GRCop-84 (Cu-8Cr-4Nb), so it trades some cold strength for conductivity and easier powder making [1]. In a printed part, build and heat treatment move the properties as much as the alloy does [4] [2] [5].

Introduction

Why the question matters and what was read to answer it.

GRCop-42 and GRCop-84 are NASA copper alloys hardened by precipitates of Cr2Nb, a chromium-niobium intermetallic compound. They were developed for the liners of regeneratively cooled rocket combustion chambers, which need high strength and high conductivity at elevated temperature [3]. For a liner, thermal conductivity and low-cycle fatigue (LCF) dominate the choice, because the constrained hot wall is strained by 1 % or more on every firing [1]. A copper liner fired with hydrogen and oxygen can also suffer blanching, surface damage from repeated oxidation and reduction of the hot wall [1]. Both alloys are now 3-D printed, by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF, a laser melting thin layers of powder) or laser-powder directed energy deposition (DED, powder blown into a moving laser spot), and the parts are usually hot isostatically pressed (HIP, held hot under high gas pressure) to close porosity [5]. Electrical conductivity is quoted as a percent of the International Annealed Copper Standard (% IACS, where pure annealed copper is 100 %). The open question is how much of the published difference between the two alloys survives in printed parts at service temperature.

6 papers were read for this summary, found through 5 searches (OpenAlex). 4 more papers judged relevant could not be obtained; they are listed at the end with their DOIs. (a) Which alloy is stronger, and does the gap last at service temperature?

GRCop-84 is stronger cold, the gap closes at service temperature

Where the numbers come from
Method
Values typed from the papers' tables and text, each checked against the printed page; curves were read from the paper's vector drawing: the axis ticks were located in the drawing itself, their values read off the figure, each label checked to sit on its tick, and the drawn curve converted to numbers at fixed temperature steps.
Inputs
Gradl 2019 [1]: Fig. 6, digitized; Table 3; Fig. 7, digitized; Mahtabi 2025 [6]: text, p. 9979; Seltzman & Wukitch 2021 [2]: Table 6; Demeneghi 2025 [5]: text (Discussion)
Tells us
GRCop-84 is stronger cold; the wrought curves meet near 600 °C, and printed room-temperature values spread by more than the gap between the alloys.
Assumes
wrought curves stand in for printed material above room temperature; a digitized point is the authors' drawn curve, not a single test
  • Wrought: "At lower temperatures, mainly below 600 °C (1112 °F), GRCop-84 is clearly stronger than GRCop-42", and the two have "essentially the same strength" above 600 °C [1].
  • HIPed L-PBF at room temperature (ASTM E8): GRCop-84 56.6 ksi UTS / 30.2 ksi yield / 30 % elongation; GRCop-42 52 ksi / 25.1 ksi / 32.2 % on the same Concept Laser M2 [1].
  • L-PBF from multiple vendors, room temperature: GRCop-84 444 ± 2 MPa UTS and 256 ± 7 MPa yield, GRCop-42 348 ± 11 MPa and 200 ± 6 MPa, with GRCop-42 more ductile (37.4 % against 34.4 % elongation) [6]. That paper does not state the specimens' heat treatment in the sections read, so these numbers should not be set beside the HIPed values above as if the condition matched.
  • Wrought LCF at room temperature, 400 and 600 °C: GRCop-42 always ran a lower stress range than GRCop-84 at the same strain, with similar plastic strain per cycle [1]. No LCF comparison of printed material was found in this review.

(b) How much more heat does GRCop-42 conduct?

GRCop-42 wins, by an amount the sources disagree on

Where the numbers come from
Method
Values typed from the papers' tables and text, each checked against the printed page; curves digitized as described in (a).
Inputs
Gradl 2019 [1]: Fig. 1, digitized; Fig. 15, digitized; Chen 2023 [3]: text (Section 3.1 fit)
Tells us
GRCop-42 conducts 13 to 20 % more than standard wrought GRCop-84 and 5 to 8 % more in the early printed samples; iron content and the build move the numbers by as much.
Assumes
a digitized point is the authors' drawn curve, not a single test
  • Wrought, room temperature: 85 % IACS for GRCop-42 against 75 % IACS for GRCop-84 [1].
  • L-PBF, laser flash: "a 5-8% increase in conductivity" for GRCop-42 over GRCop-84 on early development samples [1].
  • Two later papers state the advantage as "about 15 %" [3] and "about 10 %" [5]. Both figures are relayed from earlier references in their introductions, not measured in those papers.
  • L-PBF GRCop-42 from eight vendors fits k(T) = -39.86 T^3 + 4.17 T^2 + 0.97 T + 329.14 W/(m K), T in K/1000, over 25-700 °C, with about ±4 % scatter against ±3 % instrument uncertainty [3]. The same study's two GRCop-84 plates are plotted but not tabulated, so this review has no same-study ratio.
  • Thermal expansion: GRCop-42 expands more than GRCop-84 because it has less Cr2Nb [1]; printed GRCop-84 expands less than extruded GRCop-84 [3].

(c) Do process and heat treatment matter as much as the alloy?

GRCop-42 prints faster; both are sensitive to process and heat treatment

Where the numbers come from
Method
Values typed from the papers' tables and text, each checked against the printed page.
Inputs
Gradl 2019 [1]: Table 3; Mahtabi 2025 [6]: text, p. 9979; Seltzman & Wukitch 2021 [2]: Table 6; Table 7; Demeneghi 2024 [4]: Table 3
Tells us
Heat treatment moves printed GRCop-84 from about 210 to 785 MPa yield; at a matched condition the two alloys differ by 25 to 60 MPa.
Assumes
the papers' own numbers are right
  • GRCop-42 parameters cut L-PBF build time by about 20 % relative to GRCop-84, whose 30 µm layers could take 28 days for a 10.8 in part; GRCop-42 powder was easier for vendors to make to specification [1].
  • L-PBF and DED give different GRCop-42 properties: thin walls showed 145-165 MPa yield by L-PBF against 101-102 MPa by DED, with more lack-of-fusion porosity in L-PBF and interlayer separation in DED [4].
  • Heat treatment dominates GRCop-84 strength. Bars printed and tested across the build direction gave 492 MPa yield / 742 MPa UTS as printed, 291 / 520 MPa after 900 °C for 5 h, and 785 ± 36 / 970 MPa after 450 °C for 3 h, all at room temperature [2]. These are the paper's Tables 6 and 7 (the 450 °C value is the mean of four bars); its abstract rounds them to 500, 300 and 810 MPa.
  • One L-PBF GRCop-42 chamber split on its 10th start at build-interruption witness lines, where post-HIP porosity reached 1.9 % and oxygen (0.06 wt%) exceeded the 0.050 wt% maximum; deliberately interrupted test bars were sound, so the authors fault process control, not the alloy [5].

Caveats and next steps

What this search did not settle.

This review found no same-study, same-condition comparison of printed GRCop-42 and GRCop-84 at service temperature (500-800 °C) for creep or LCF, and no hot-fire test of the two alloys in one campaign. The NASA statements that the alloys match above 600 °C and differ in LCF stress range rest on wrought material [1]. Four relevant papers could not be obtained and are listed at the end, including a 2021 NASA paper on the hot-fire durability of post-processed printed GRCop chambers, which may cover this gap.
