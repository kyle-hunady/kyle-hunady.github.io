Short-range order outlasts long-range order. α1 is the Warren–Cowley parameter for nearest neighbours. It is −1 for perfect unlike pairing and 0 for random mixing. Above Tc it stays well away from zero, while mean-field theory (dashed) says it vanishes.

What you’re seeing

Each site holds an A or a B atom, half and half. Every step picks a neighbouring pair and swaps it if the energy drops. If the energy would rise, the swap still happens with probability exp(−ΔE/kT). That is the Metropolis rule, and swapping keeps the composition fixed. Temperature is in units of Tc, the temperature where long-range order disappears. For this lattice kTc = 2.269 J, which Onsager worked out exactly.

Below Tc the grid divides into two sublattices and A prefers one of them. The order parameter η measures how completely. It is 1 for a perfect checkerboard and falls to zero at Tc. Above Tc an A atom still has more B neighbours than chance would give it, but the pattern does not carry across the grid. That leftover is short-range order, and α1 measures it. A quench shows a different failure: the atoms order locally in several patches whose checkerboards are shifted against each other, and η stays small.

A powder X-ray pattern reports on the same thing. Peaks from the average lattice have structure factor F = fA + fB and do not depend on order. Superlattice peaks have F = η(fA − fB), so their intensity goes as η2(fA − fB)2. Fe and Al differ by a factor of two in scattering power, so the peaks are clear. Cu and Zn differ by one electron, which makes those peaks roughly 400 times weaker, and a laboratory pattern shows almost nothing. Neutrons, or X-rays tuned near an absorption edge, see a larger difference between such neighbours.

Where it shows up

β-brass (Cu–Zn near 50–50) orders into B2 below about 460 °C, and Fe–Al near equal composition forms B2 order on cooling. Textbook treatments are in Porter, Easterling and Sherif, and in Cullity and Stock.
