Faster entropy estimates with a downloaded force field

Camille Bernal-Choban’s thesis computed the entropy of melting with a potential fitted to each element. Here the same workflow runs on MACE-OMAT-0, a pretrained potential anyone can download, and every result is checked against hers.

Question. Camille Bernal-Choban’s 2024 Caltech thesis (Bernal-Choban et al. 2024) measured and computed the entropy that lead, bismuth, tin, germanium and silicon gain on melting, with a potential fitted to each element. Does MACE-OMAT-0, fitted to none of them, give her numbers?
Answer so far. Partly. The entropy of fusion of germanium, silicon and bismuth lands within 4 % of experiment, as close as her fitted potentials, but lead comes out 26 % high, against her 8 %. Melting points mostly come out low: germanium 32 % (hers 19 %), silicon 14 % (hers also 14 %), bismuth 18 %. Lead’s is 2 % off, against her 10 %. The electronic part is smaller than hers for all three elements she computed, eight times smaller for lead. Tin cannot be tested, because the model’s crystal falls apart before it melts.

ISame workflow, one swap

Fitting a machine-learned potential for a new element takes hundreds of DFT calculations and weeks of work. A pretrained potential such as MACE-OMAT-0 is trained once across the periodic table and anyone can download it. If one is good enough, a new material skips the fitting step. To test that, I am repeating Bernal-Choban’s melting study with her per-element moment tensor potentials (MTP; Shapeev 2016) replaced by the downloaded model. Her numbers come from our own group, so each difference can be traced to its cause.

The targets: her measured entropies

The entropy gained on melting has three parts: vibrational (softer vibrations in the liquid), configurational (the many arrangements a liquid moves among) and electronic (electrons excited near the Fermi level). Bernal-Choban measured the vibrational part by inelastic neutron scattering, computed the electronic part with DFT on simulation snapshots, and took the configurational part as what remains. Entropies are in Boltzmann’s constant per atom (1 kB per atom ≈ 8.3 J/(mol·K)).

The semiconductors gain over three times the entropy of a simple metal, and for lead almost all of it is configurational.

IIMelting points come out low

First check: a real crystal and a real liquid

A model has its own melting point, so every MACE number is taken at the model’s Tm, not the measured one. Before looking for it, the model has to make a real crystal and a real liquid.

crystalliquid

simulationMACE keeps a proper crystal and liquid for lead and germanium, so its melting points are worth reading. Replayed from saved MACE trajectories, both phases at one temperature and zero pressure. Lead keeps about the same number of neighbors when it melts. Germanium goes from four bonded neighbors to almost six and gets denser, as ice does. The lower plot is the pair distribution g(r), the relative chance of finding another atom at distance r.

Lead melts at 611 K in the model

Heating a perfect crystal overshoots Tm, because a periodic crystal has no surface where melting can start. I use the coexistence method instead (Morris et al. 1994): a long box, half crystal and half liquid, held at constant temperature and pressure. Above the model’s Tm the liquid grows; below it the crystal grows. Each atom is labeled crystal-like or liquid-like by the symmetry of its neighbor shell (q̄6; Steinhardt et al. 1983; Lechner & Dellago 2008).

crystal-like atomliquid-like atom

simulationEach slab run says only “above” or “below” the model’s melting point: at 590 K the crystal grows, at 663 K it melts within 47 ps. Each box holds 1,024 lead atoms (2 nm by 8 nm), saved every 0.5 ps and interpolated between frames.

The slope of the crystal fraction against time gives the speed of the crystal–liquid boundary. I bracketed lead in three rounds of narrower, longer runs (up to 280 ps, two seeds each). The speed changes sign between 605 and 620 K, and interpolating the seed averages to zero gives 611 ± 3 K.

IIIEntropy of fusion: close except lead

ΔGfus = ΔHfus − T ΔSfus
at Tm:  ΔGfus = 0  ⇒  ΔSfus = ΔHfus / Tm

The entropy of fusion is the latent heat divided by the melting point, both from the same model, so it can survive a wrong melting point. The latent heat is the energy difference between long crystal and liquid runs at the model’s Tm. For germanium (3.57 kB per atom), silicon (3.44) and bismuth (2.47) it is low by about the same fraction as the melting point, and the ratio lands within 4 % of experiment. For lead the melting point is right and the latent heat is too large: 1.20 ± 0.05 kB per atom, against 0.95 measured and 1.03 from her potential.

IVElectronic part comes out smaller

The electronic entropy of a snapshot follows from its electronic density of states g(E) and the Fermi function f: Sel = −kB ∫ g(E) [f ln f + (1 − f) ln(1 − f)] dE (Fultz 2010), so only states within a few kBT of the Fermi level count. I compute g(E) with DFT (VASP, Kresse & Furthmüller 1996; PBE, Perdew et al. 1996) on snapshots of the MACE crystal and liquid, as she did on hers.

crystalliquidFermi level

simulationMelting fills the gap at the Fermi level in germanium and silicon and barely changes lead, which is why lead’s electronic part is small. What counts is the height of each curve in the light band at E = 0. Faint lines are single MACE snapshots, thick lines their average, at the measured melting points.

For lead the liquid-minus-crystal difference is 0.0082 ± 0.0005 kB per atom at 611 K, against 0.07 in the thesis. The k-point mesh, smearing and reference structure each move it by at most 0.015, so the gap is not a convergence problem. Germanium and silicon come out at 0.10 and 0.16, against her 0.16 and 0.19, partly because the model melts them 386 K and 233 K colder and electronic entropy grows with temperature (at the measured melting points they rise to 0.13 and 0.17). For bismuth, which the thesis does not cover, a first estimate with spin–orbit coupling at the measured melting point is 0.08.

Background: melting as a balance, and what MACE sees

Melting is a balance of heat and entropy

At fixed temperature and pressure the phase with the lower Gibbs free energy, G = H − TS, is stable. The liquid has the higher enthalpy, because breaking the crystal costs energy, but also the higher entropy. Heating makes the −TS term count for more until the two free energies cross at Tm.

crystalliquidmelting point

modelA model with too much latent heat for its entropy melts too high, and the reverse. The starting values are lead’s, 4.8 kJ/mol and 0.95 kB per atom, which put the crossing near 600 K.

Why not use DFT directly?

One DFT calculation on a 128-atom lead snapshot takes about 85 seconds on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Finding a melting point needs hundreds of thousands of time steps on about a thousand atoms, which at DFT speed would take years. MACE takes about 36 milliseconds per step for a 108-atom lead cell on the same GPU, more than 2,000 times faster. DFT is still used where it matters, for the electronic part.

What MACE sees

MACE (Batatia et al. 2022) writes the energy as a sum over atoms, each depending only on the atoms within 6 Å. It is a graph neural network: each pair inside the cutoff is an edge, and in each of two layers every atom collects direction- and distance-aware messages from its neighbors, so information travels up to 12 Å. MACE-OMAT-0 was trained on Meta’s OMat24 set (Barroso-Luque et al. 2024), over 100 million DFT calculations that include distorted, rattled and high-temperature structures, which is what makes liquids worth trying.

modelMACE reads 18 to 34 neighbors directly, depending on how the crystal packs. Perfect crystals at their measured room-temperature lattices: close-packed lead, open diamond germanium and layered bismuth. The gold sphere is the 6 Å cutoff around the white atom; the bright atoms inside it are the ones the model reads.

What I did

  • Ran MACE molecular dynamics of crystals, liquids and 1,000-atom coexistence cells on NERSC Perlmutter GPUs, with pass/fail criteria written down before the runs.
  • Computed melting points and entropies of fusion from the model’s own runs.
  • Ran DFT (VASP) on MD snapshots for the electronic part, with convergence tests.
  • Checked every thesis value against her tables.
