Faster entropy estimates with a downloaded force field

Pretrained machine-learned potentials, built from databases that already exist, can estimate thermodynamic properties without fitting a new model for every material. I test how close one gets to the entropy of melting, with lead as the worked example.

Machine-learned interatomic potentials let a computer follow thousands of atoms with close to quantum-mechanical accuracy. Until recently every study fitted its own: hundreds of quantum-mechanical calculations per element, an active-learning loop, and weeks of work before the first useful simulation. Pretrained “foundation” potentials change that. Models such as MACE-OMAT-0 are trained once on over a hundred million calculations across the periodic table, and anyone can download them and use them as they are. If they are accurate enough, every new material skips the fitting step. The goal is not a perfect number but a good estimate, fast, from tools that already exist, so the expensive calculations go only where the estimate is not good enough.

To find out how far that goes, I am repeating a melting study from our own group with a downloaded model. a 2024 Caltech PhD thesis by Camille Bernal-Choban, a former member of our group (Bernal-Choban et al. 2024) measured how much entropy several elements gain when they melt, split it into its sources, and backed the split with simulations that used potentials fitted to each element. Because the reference numbers and the people who made them are in the same lab, every difference can be traced to its cause. Lead is the worked example: MACE melts it within 2 % of the measured temperature, but its entropy of fusion comes out about 25 % high, and its electronic part is about eight times smaller than hers. On bismuth, zinc, germanium and silicon the model melts 14–34 % too early, yet the entropy of fusion lands within 10 % of the measured value. Results are updated as they come in; preliminary numbers are labelled.

ΔGfus = ΔHfus − T ΔSfus
at Tm:  ΔGfus = 0  ⇒  ΔSfus = ΔHfus / Tm

The crystal melts where the liquid’s free energy drops below the crystal’s. A potential has to get both the latent heat ΔHfus and the melting point Tm right to get the entropy of fusion ΔSfus right; everything on this page tests one of those three.

IThe test

Melting is a balance of heat and entropy

At fixed temperature and pressure the phase with the lower Gibbs free energy, G = H − TS, is stable. The liquid has the higher enthalpy H, because breaking the crystal costs energy, but also the higher entropy S. Heating makes the −TS term count for more until the two free energies cross at the melting point Tm. There the latent heat L and the entropy of fusion are tied together: ΔSfus = L / Tm. I give entropy in units of Boltzmann’s constant per atom (1 kB per atom ≈ 8.3 J/(mol·K)).

modelThe liquid wins where its line drops below the crystal’s. The starting values are lead’s: 4.8 kJ/mol and 0.95 kB per atom put the crossing near 600 K. Raising the entropy of fusion lowers the melting point for the same latent heat.

Three sources of entropy

Many metals gain close to 1 kB per atom on melting (Richards’ rule, 1897); bismuth, germanium and silicon gain two to three times that. The gain has three parts. Vibrational: atoms in a liquid still vibrate, and softer, lower-frequency vibrations carry more entropy. Configurational: a crystal has one arrangement of sites, a liquid moves among a huge number. Electronic: electrons near the Fermi level can be thermally excited, and the more states there are, the more entropy. Bernal-Choban measured the vibrational part by inelastic neutron scattering at the Spallation Neutron Source, computed the electronic part with density functional theory on simulation snapshots, and took the configurational part as what remains.

For Pb, Ge and Si the bar is split into Bernal-Choban’s vibrational (rose), electronic (green) and configurational (lilac) parts; untick the box for totals only. For lead the vibrational part is slightly negative and the configurational part is almost all of the total.

That workflow has three steps a potential has to get right: the melting point, the latent heat, and atomic structures realistic enough for the electronic calculation. She used moment tensor potentials (Shapeev 2016) fitted separately for each element. The question here is whether a model nobody fitted to these elements gives the same answers.

IIFitted and downloaded potentials

Why not use quantum mechanics directly?

Density functional theory (DFT) computes the energy and forces of a set of atoms from their electrons. It is accurate enough for this work, but slow: one DFT calculation on a 128-atom lead snapshot takes about 85 seconds on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. Finding a melting point needs hundreds of thousands of time steps on about a thousand atoms, which at DFT speed would take years. A machine-learned potential is fitted to many DFT calculations and then predicts energies and forces in milliseconds. MACE takes about 36 milliseconds per time step for a 108-atom lead cell on the same GPU, more than 2,000 times faster. DFT is still used where it matters: the electronic entropy comes from DFT on snapshots taken from the MACE runs.

What MACE sees

MACE (Batatia et al. 2022) writes the total energy as a sum over atoms. Each atom’s energy depends only on the atoms within a cutoff of 6 Å. The model is a graph neural network: atoms are nodes and each pair inside the cutoff is an edge. In each layer every atom collects messages from its neighbours that encode their directions and distances, including combinations of up to four atoms at once. The messages respect rotation symmetry, so rotating the atoms rotates the forces in the same way. After two layers an atom has information from atoms up to 12 Å away.

modelMACE reads 18 to 34 neighbours directly, depending on how the crystal packs. Each panel is a perfect crystal at its measured room-temperature lattice: close-packed fcc lead, open diamond germanium and layered A7 bismuth. The gold sphere is the 6 Å cutoff around the white atom; the bright atoms inside it are the ones the model reads. Diamond germanium fits more atoms inside 6 Å than lead because its atoms sit closer together, even though each has only four bonded neighbours. Tick the box to see how far information travels after the second layer.

MACE-OMAT-0 was trained on Meta’s OMat24 set (Barroso-Luque et al. 2024), over 100 million DFT calculations that deliberately include distorted, rattled and high-temperature structures. Liquids are far from the perfect crystals in most training sets, so that breadth is what makes melting worth trying. Nothing in it was fitted to the elements on this page.

Where does the model melt?

A force field is a model with its own physics, including its own melting point. Comparing entropies at the measured temperature would mix two errors, so every quantity here is evaluated at the model’s melting point. Heating a perfect crystal until it melts overshoots Tm: a periodic crystal has no surface where melting can start. Instead I use the coexistence method (Morris et al. 1994): a long box, half crystal and half liquid, held at constant temperature and pressure. Above the model’s Tm the liquid grows; below it the crystal grows. To tell the phases apart, each atom is labelled by the symmetry of its neighbour shell, the averaged bond-order parameter q̄6 (Steinhardt et al. 1983; Lechner & Dellago 2008).

simulationAt 590 K the crystal grows; at 663 K it melts completely within 47 ps. Each box holds 1,024 lead atoms, 2 nm wide and 8 nm long, simulated with MACE and saved every 0.5 ps; the motion between saved frames is interpolated. Drag either slab to rotate both; atoms crossing a face of the periodic box fade across to the opposite side. Gold atoms have crystal-like surroundings, measured by a bond-orientation parameter (averaged q6); blue atoms have liquid-like surroundings. More runs between these temperatures put lead’s model melting point at 611 ± 3 K. The measured value is 600.6 K.

Each slab run gives one number: how fast the crystal–liquid boundary moves, from the slope of the crystal fraction against time (lower panel above). Positive means the crystal grows, so the run was below the model’s Tm; negative means it melts. I bracketed lead in three rounds, each narrower and longer than the last:

  • Round 1. Wide scan, one seed each, 35 ps (540, 601 and 663 K): the crystal grows at 540 K and melts at 663 K, so Tm lies between.
  • Round 2. Two seeds at 590 and 620 K, 140 ps each: 590 K grows on both seeds, 620 K shrinks on both but too slowly to call.
  • Round 3. Two seeds at 605 and 625 K, 280 ps each: 625 K melts on both, 605 K still drifts slightly toward growth.

The velocity changes sign between 605 and 620 K. Averaging the two seeds at each temperature and interpolating linearly to zero gives the model’s melting point, 611 ± 3 K; the uncertainty is the spread over reasonable choices of which runs to fit.

Entropy of fusion

At the model’s melting point the entropy of fusion follows from the model’s own latent heat: the energy difference between long crystal and liquid runs at Tm, divided by Tm. For lead this gives 1.20 ± 0.05 kB per atom, against 0.95 measured and 1.03 from Bernal-Choban’s fitted potential. MACE’s latent heat is too large for its melting point, so its liquid gains about 25 % too much entropy.

The electronic part

The electronic entropy of a snapshot follows from its electronic density of states g(E) and the Fermi function f: Sel = −kB ∫ g(E) [f ln f + (1 − f) ln(1 − f)] dE (Fultz 2010). Only states within a few kBT of the Fermi level contribute. I compute g(E) with DFT (VASP, Kresse & Furthmüller 1996; PBE functional, Perdew et al. 1996) on snapshots of the MACE crystal and liquid, and average over snapshots.

For lead the difference between liquid and crystal is 0.0082 ± 0.0005 kB per atom at 611 K, against 0.07 in Bernal-Choban’s thesis. Denser k-point meshes, smearing and the reference structure each move the number by at most 0.015, so the gap is not a convergence problem. Its cause is the open question for lead: the snapshots come from a different potential, at a different temperature, and her thesis may define the reference differently. I am testing each in turn.

The entropy of fusion holds up where the melting point does not. MACE melts germanium about 30 % too low, but its latent heat is low by a similar fraction, so the ratio stays near the measured 3.67 kB per atom. The electronic part comes out below Bernal-Choban’s values for germanium and silicon too: 0.10 and 0.16 kB per atom at the model melting points, against 0.16 and 0.19 in her thesis. Part of that is temperature, since the model melts them 386 K and 237 K colder and electronic entropy grows with temperature. For bismuth her thesis had no electronic value; the first estimate here is 0.08 kB per atom, about 3 % of its entropy of fusion. Spin–orbit coupling matters there: without it the crystal’s electronic entropy is overestimated by a factor of about 2.5.

VWhat this shows

  • A free, pretrained potential reproduces the shape of Bernal-Choban’s results with no fitting: realistic crystals and liquids, and entropies of fusion within 10 % for four of five elements it can melt.
  • Its errors are systematic rather than random. Covalent germanium and silicon, semimetal bismuth and hexagonal zinc all melt too early; lead melts on time but with too much latent heat; β-tin is not stable at all. These are the cases where a fitted model would still be needed.
  • The electronic part is smaller than Bernal-Choban’s value for every element compared. For lead the factor is eight and the reason is not yet known.

Next

  • Compute the vibrational part from the velocity autocorrelation of the same runs, then the configurational part by difference, as Bernal-Choban did.
  • Trace the lead electronic-entropy gap to its cause.

What I did

  • Ran MACE molecular dynamics of crystals, liquids and 1,000-atom solid–liquid coexistence cells on NERSC Perlmutter A100 GPUs.
  • Bracketed each model melting point with seeded coexistence runs and pass/fail criteria written down before the runs.
  • Computed entropies of fusion from the model’s own latent heat and melting point.
  • Ran DFT (VASP) on MD snapshots for the electronic density of states, with k-point, smearing and spin–orbit convergence tests.
  • Wrote the analysis and the figures on this page from the simulation output.
